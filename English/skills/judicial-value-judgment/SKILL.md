## Capabilities

- Determine whether the case truly requires value judgment, rather than mechanical application of legal rules.
- Distinguish issues of fact-finding, legal interpretation, value weighing, and discretionary choice.
- Identify core legal values such as order, freedom, equality, human rights, justice, and efficiency.
- Analyze value conflicts between individuals, between communities, and between individuals and communities.
- Apply the value-hierarchy principle, case-balancing principle, proportionality principle, and fundamental-interests-of-the-people principle to address value conflicts.
- Compare alternative discretionary schemes on normative basis, value orientation, interest impact, and adjudicative risk.
- Generate value-judgment analytical opinions that judges can rewrite into adjudicative reasoning.

## How to Use

### Step 1: Decide Whether to Invoke This Skill

First determine whether the case presents a genuine value-judgment task.

Check:

1. Whether legal rules are uncertain;
2. Whether there is conflict among rights, interests, or values;
3. Whether there are open-textured legal concepts, discretionary space, or normative gaps;
4. Whether a rights limitation, sacrifice of interests, or public-purpose measure must be reviewed for proportionality;
5. Whether mechanical rule application would produce clear injustice or inadequate reasoning.

If the case involves only pure fact-finding, evidence evaluation, or clear rule application, do not force invocation of this Skill.

### Step 2: Lock the Discretionary Issue

Extract from the facts the question the judge truly must decide.

Distinguish:

1. Fact issues: whether facts occurred; whether evidence suffices to prove them;
2. Normative issues: which legal rule applies; how the rule’s meaning is interpreted;
3. Value issues: how to choose among competing rights, interests, or values;
4. Discretionary issues: how to choose the more justified scheme among multiple lawful options.

This Skill focuses on categories 3 and 4.  
If fact or normative issues remain unresolved, first note their impact on value judgment; do not draw definite conclusions prematurely.

### Step 3: Identify the Structure of the Value Conflict

Classify the conflict in this case:

1. Conflicts between individuals: e.g., one party’s free exercise of rights versus another’s personality, property, privacy, reputation, or other interests;
2. Conflicts between communities: e.g., conflicts among different public interests, institutional goals, regional interests, or collective interests;
3. Conflicts between individuals and communities: e.g., personal freedom, property rights, or privacy versus public order, public safety, or administrative efficiency.

Output should clearly state:

- Parties to the conflict;
- Values in conflict;
- How the conflict manifests;
- Whose interests are limited or diminished;
- Whether the conflict is real, or only apparent and resolvable by interpretation, factual distinction, or institutional design.

### Step 4: Locate Relevant Legal Values

Identify values implicated in this case from the following core legal values:

1. Order: stable social relations, orderly exercise of power, fewer disputes, possibility of social cooperation;
2. Freedom: lawful autonomy of subjects to choose and act, free from unlawful interference;
3. Equality: exclusion of privilege and discrimination; formal equality and necessary substantive equality;
4. Human rights: respect for independent personality, equal dignity, and basic rights;
5. Justice: just allocation of benefits, burdens, responsibilities, and procedure;
6. Efficiency: institutional arrangements that promote resource allocation, convenience of activity, and growth of social wealth.

Do not list values generically. For each value, explain:

- How it concretely appears in this case;
- Which facts and norms support it;
- What limitation, threat, or harm it faces;
- Precisely where it conflicts with another value.

### Step 5: Confirm Normative Basis and Boundaries of Discretion

Value judgment must proceed within the legal framework; do not make pure moral judgments detached from norms.

Check:

1. Whether clear legal rules exist;
2. Whether legal principles, institutional purposes, or legislative purposes exist;
3. Whether the Constitution, statutes, judicial interpretations, Guiding Cases, or similar-case adjudicative rules exist;
4. Whether the judge has discretionary space;
5. Whether value judgment might exceed the legal text, institutional purpose, or procedural limits;
6. Whether there are untouchable basic rights, statutory procedures, or jurisdictional boundaries.

If the normative basis is insufficient, mark: “It is inappropriate to draw a definitive value conclusion at this stage.”

### Step 6: Apply the Value-Hierarchy Principle

When legal values of different ranks conflict, preferentially protect the higher-ranked value.

Judgment rules:

1. Basic values generally take priority over non-basic values;
2. Human rights and justice carry higher argumentative weight in rights protection and rule-of-law evaluation;
3. Order, efficiency, and administrative convenience must not be generalized into reasons that automatically override basic rights;
4. Even when a higher value has priority, necessity and limits of restricting other values must still be explained;
5. Abstract value hierarchy must not replace case-specific argumentation.

Output should state:

- Which values carry higher weight;
- Factual and normative reasons for that weighting;
- Whether restricted values receive minimum protection;
- Whether the hierarchy judgment is contested.

### Step 7: Apply the Case-Balancing Principle

When values of the same rank, or values hard to rank, conflict, conduct case balancing.

Consider comprehensively:

1. Parties’ status, conduct, and subjective state;
2. Degree to which rights or interests are affected;
3. Whether harm is real, concrete, and serious;
4. Whether alternative arrangements exist;
5. Whether protecting one side would impose excessive burden on the other;
6. Impact of the discretionary outcome on similar cases, social order, and institutional operation;
7. Whether both sides’ legitimate interests can be accommodated.

Case balancing is not simple compromise; it seeks, on factual and normative grounds, the discretionary scheme that better accommodates legitimate interests.

### Step 8: Apply the Proportionality Principle When Necessary

When the case involves “limiting one legal interest to protect a superior legal value,” apply proportionality. Especially applicable where public power limits individual rights, public interest limits private interests, or institutional goals limit individual freedom.

Review in sequence:

1. Legitimate aim: whether the restrictive measure pursues a lawful, legitimate, and important legal purpose;
2. Suitability: whether the measure is conducive to achieving the purpose;
3. Necessity: whether equally effective but less harmful alternatives exist;
4. Narrow proportionality (balancing in the strict sense): whether the public interest, institutional interest, or rights-protection interest obtained clearly outweighs the harm to the other party’s rights or interests.

Do not equate “legitimate aim” with “reasonable restriction.”

### Step 9: Apply the Fundamental-Interests-of-the-People Principle When Necessary

In major hard cases where value conflict cannot be resolved by hierarchy, case balancing, or proportionality alone, also consider whether the discretionary scheme accords with the fundamental interests of the people.

Usage rules:

1. Do not use “fundamental interests of the people” as an empty slogan;
2. Concretely state the social groups, institutional goals, and real-world impacts corresponding to that interest;
3. Respect individual rights protection at the same time; avoid overriding individual basic rights with abstract collective interest;
4. Explain how this principle supplements rather than replaces legal rules, value hierarchy, and proportionality.

### Step 10: Compare Discretionary Schemes

Compare at least two optional discretionary schemes.  
If only one scheme is clearly adoptable, still explain why other schemes are not.

For each scheme analyze:

1. Core values it supports;
2. Values sacrificed or limited;
3. Normative basis;
4. Factual basis;
5. Advantages;
6. Risks;
7. Possible rebuttal points;
8. Consistency with value hierarchy, case balancing, and proportionality.

### Step 11: Form a Framework for Adjudicative Reasoning

Finally generate a reasoning framework the judge can rewrite.

Reasoning should include:

1. Why this case requires value judgment;
2. What value conflict exists;
3. Why the court chooses a particular value-judgment path;
4. Whether restricted values receive necessary respect;
5. How the discretionary conclusion accords with normative purpose, case justice, and proportionality;
6. How uncertainties and opposing views are addressed.

## Input Format

Acceptable inputs include:

- Case summary: facts, dispute background, party relationships;
- Issues in dispute: questions the judge must decide;
- Discretionary needs: whether rights-conflict analysis, legal-uncertainty analysis, proportionality review, or adjudicative-reasoning assistance is needed.

Minimum input should include:

1. Basic facts;
2. Issues in dispute;
3. Rights, interests, or values implicated;
4. The question requiring discretion.

When information is insufficient:

1. First ask clarifying questions for critical gaps that block further analysis;
2. For matters that may be temporarily assumed, expressly state “on the premise that the following assumptions hold”;
3. Do not invent unverifiable facts, norms, or value weights;
4. For multiple possible interpretations, list how each affects the conclusion.

## Example Usage

### Example Request

```text
Please assist the judge with value judgment based on the following facts:

To address nighttime noise and traffic congestion, a city issued a normative document requiring all open-air night-market stalls in the core downtown commercial district to cease business after 22:00 each day. Zhang has long operated a late-night food stall; most of his revenue comes from 22:00 to 2:00 a.m. Zhang argues the rule severely impairs his freedom of business and livelihood and asks the court to review whether the restriction is reasonable. The municipal government asserts the measure maintains public order, protects nearby residents’ right to rest, and improves traffic safety.

Please analyze the value conflict among freedom of business, public order, residents’ right to rest, and administrative efficiency, and provide a proportionality review and adjudicative-reasoning framework.

```

### Example Output

```markdown
# Judicial Value Judgment Analytical Opinion

## I. Locating the Discretionary Issue

This case requires value judgment. The dispute is not pure fact-finding; it concerns whether, when an administrative organ limits operators’ freedom of business by time in order to maintain public order, residents’ right to rest, and traffic safety, that limitation is legitimate and proportionate.

The issue to decide: whether requiring night-market stalls in the core commercial district to cease after 22:00 maintains a reasonable balance between protecting public interests and limiting individual freedom of business.

## II. Identifying the Value Conflict

- Conflict type: Value conflict between individuals and the community, also including conflict of individual interests between operators and nearby residents.
- Parties to the conflict: Night-market operators; nearby residents; the municipal government and public-management order.
- Values in conflict:
  - Operators: freedom of business, livelihood interests, property-related interests;
  - Residents: right to rest, peace of living, health interests;
  - Public management: public order, traffic safety, administrative efficiency.
- Manifestation: Continued night-market operation may affect residents’ rest and traffic order; a uniform 22:00 closure may significantly affect operators’ income.

## III. Analysis of Relevant Legal Values

| Legal Value | Concrete Manifestation in This Case | Supporting Facts/Norms | Degree of Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freedom | Operators’ freedom to choose business hours and methods | Zhang’s main income is from 22:00 to 2:00 a.m. | Significantly affected |
| Order | Nighttime public-space order, traffic order, noise control | Municipal government addressing nighttime noise and congestion | Strong protection need |
| Human rights / personality interests | Residents’ rest, health, peace of living | Nearby residents’ nighttime living interests | High protection value |
| Efficiency | Uniform time standard lowers enforcement cost | Uniform 22:00 closure is easy to administer | Some value, but cannot alone override basic interests |
| Justice | Fair allocation of burdens between operators’ livelihood and residents’ peace | Both sides’ interests are legitimate | Requires case balancing |

## IV. Normative Basis and Boundaries of Discretion

Administrative organs may reasonably limit business activities for purposes such as public order, noise control, and traffic safety. The boundary of discretion is: limitations must not lack a factual basis; administrative convenience alone must not unduly restrict freedom of business; and less harmful alternatives such as zoning, time differentiation, noise limits, and licensing management should be considered.

## V. Value Hierarchy and Case Balancing

Residents’ rest and health interests and public safety carry higher protection weight; freedom of business and livelihood interests must also be respected. Public order does not automatically override freedom of business; the key is whether the measure is necessary, whether it is excessive, and whether reasonable operating space remains.

Case balancing should focus on: the actual degree of nighttime noise and congestion; whether the core commercial district truly needs special management; whether 22:00 as a uniform closing time is too early; whether alternatives such as zoning or noise limits exist; and whether transition periods or alternative arrangements are provided for operators.

## VI. Proportionality Review

- Whether proportionality review is needed: Yes. The case involves limitation of individual freedom of business for public-management purposes.

1. Legitimate aim: Controlling noise, maintaining traffic safety, and protecting residents’ right to rest are legitimate public purposes.
2. Suitability: Restricting night markets after 22:00 helps reduce nighttime crowds, noise, and traffic pressure; it has some suitability.
3. Necessity: If less harmful alternatives exist—e.g., designated night-market zones, noise standards, different hours on weekdays vs. holidays—a uniform 22:00 closure may fail necessity.
4. Narrow proportionality: If the measure largely eliminates operators’ main income source, while the administration neither shows that uniform early closure is required nor provides buffers or alternatives, public-interest gains may be out of balance with individual harm.

## VII. Review under the Fundamental-Interests-of-the-People Principle

This case usually need not rise separately to the fundamental-interests-of-the-people principle. If urban nighttime governance implicates major public safety, widespread resident health, or important public-resource allocation, it may be a supplementary consideration, but abstract public interest must not directly override operators’ lawful rights and interests.

## VIII. Comparison of Discretionary Schemes

| Scheme | Values Supported | Values Limited | Advantages | Risks | Adoptable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maintain uniform post-22:00 closure | Order, efficiency, residents’ right to rest | Freedom of business, livelihood | Simple to administer; direct governance effect | May over-restrict; “one-size-fits-all” risk | Adoptable only after strict justification |
| Require government to shift to zoning, time differentiation, and noise limits | Freedom, justice, proportionality | Administrative efficiency | Better balances operators and residents | Higher enforcement cost | Relatively adoptable |
| Fully revoke the restriction | Freedom of business | Order, residents’ right to rest | Maximally protects operators | May neglect public order and residents’ interests | Not directly adoptable |

## IX. Recommended Judgment Path

Recommend the path “legitimate aim, but means may be excessive.” The court may hold that the municipal government’s aims—controlling nighttime noise and congestion and protecting residents’ right to rest—are legitimate, yet still review whether the least harmful means were used. If the government cannot show that all open-air night-market stalls in the core commercial district must cease after 22:00, and milder alternatives exist, the rule may fail necessity and narrow proportionality.

## X. Reference for Adjudicative Reasoning

Night-market operators lawfully enjoy a measure of freedom of business and livelihood interests; nearby residents likewise enjoy peace of living, rest, and health interests. Administrative organs may reasonably manage nighttime business activities to maintain public order and traffic safety. Public order and administrative efficiency do not automatically override freedom of business. For administrative measures that restrict business activities, courts should review whether the aim is legitimate, whether the means are suitable, whether less harmful alternatives exist, and whether gains in public interest remain in balance with diminishment of individual rights and interests.

On the present facts, uniform closure after 22:00 may help achieve noise control and order maintenance, but if the administration neither shows necessity of a uniform closing time nor considers alternatives such as zoning, noise limits, holiday differentiation, or transition arrangements, the measure may impose excessive burden on operators. While respecting the administration’s governance goals, the court may require more refined, proportionate adjustment of the restrictive measure.

## XI. Risks and Rebuttal Points

- Possible rebuttal points:
  - If the government can prove severe nighttime noise and traffic problems in the area, and that other measures have failed, the reasonableness of uniform closure increases.
  - If Zhang operates without a license or unlawfully occupies roadways, the strength of protection for freedom of business decreases.
- Factual uncertainty:
  - Whether actual noise measurements and congestion data exist;
  - Whether operators’ and residents’ views were heard;
  - Whether alternative governance measures exist.
- Normative uncertainty:
  - Whether the normative document’s authority and procedure are lawful still requires separate review.
- Adjudicative risks to avoid:
  - Do not simply override operators’ interests with “public interest”;
  - Do not completely ignore residents’ right to rest and public order;
  - Do not reduce proportionality to mere review of legitimate aim.

```


## Best Practices

1.  First determine whether a genuine value-judgment problem exists; do not package every case as a value conflict.
    
2.  Always ground analysis in facts and norms; avoid value talk detached from the case.
    
3.  Every value judgment must land on concrete rights, interests, harms, and institutional purposes.
    
4.  Present both protected and restricted values; avoid one-sided steamroller argumentation.
    
5.  When using value hierarchy, explain with case facts why that value carries greater weight.
    
6.  When using proportionality, fully review legitimate aim, suitability, necessity, and narrow proportionality.
    
7.  When using the fundamental-interests-of-the-people principle, concretely state its real-world referent; do not use it as an empty slogan.
    
8.  Clearly mark uncertainty where facts are unclear, norms are ambiguous, or value conflicts are hard to rank.
    
9.  Adjudicative reasoning should remain neutral, cautious, and restrained; avoid emotional, moralizing, or policy-propaganda expression.
    
10.  Do not use abstract concepts such as efficiency, order, or public interest to automatically suppress individual basic rights.
    

## Limitations

-   This Skill cannot replace the judge’s final decision.
    
-   This Skill cannot give definite conclusions when facts are seriously unclear or basic normative bases are lacking.
    
-   This Skill does not review authenticity of evidence; it analyzes only on the basis of input materials.
    
-   This Skill is unsuitable for pure factual disputes, simple rule application, or cases requiring no discretion.
    
-   This Skill must not invent legal bases, case facts, similar-case rules, or adjudicative conclusions.
    
-   This Skill must not package personal moral preferences as legal value judgment.
    
-   This Skill must not exercise arbitrary discretion beyond the current legal framework.
    
-   Output of this Skill should serve as auxiliary material for adjudicative reasoning, not as a formal judgment document.
